### S03 · equation-steps · ~0:17

When you hit the brakes, the car slows down at a roughly constant rate. Call that deceleration a. On a dry road it's about seven meters per second, every second. The car starts at speed v and ends at zero. One of the basic equations of motion connects those, and solving it for the distance gives d equals v squared over two a.

### S04 · equation-steps · ~0:43

That little square is everything. Double v, and v squared becomes four times bigger. At fifty kilometers per hour, about fourteen meters per second, the car brakes in about fourteen meters. At one hundred kilometers per hour, about twenty eight meters per second, it needs about fifty five meters. Four times as far.

### S05 · graph · ~1:04

Here's the whole picture. Braking distance grows along a parabola. Every extra ten kilometers per hour costs more distance than the ten before it, so the curve gets steeper the faster you go.

### S06 · bullets · ~1:18

Engineers use this one line everywhere. It helps set speed limits near schools. It decides how far a driver must be able to see before a curve or a junction. And it tells car designers how much braking power a faster, heavier car needs.

### S07 · table · ~1:36

One honest detail. Before you even touch the brake pedal, you react, which takes about one and a half seconds. During that time you're still moving at full speed, and that part only doubles. So the total stopping distance grows a bit less than four times, almost three times here. But the braking part, the part physics controls, quadruples.

### S08 · answer-box · ~2:00

So remember this one line. Braking distance equals v squared over two a. Twice the speed, four times the braking distance.
